The Prisoner's Dilemma Lab

Paradigm from Rilling et al. (2002), Neuron 35:395–405

Welcome

In this exercise you'll play an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma game the same way participants did in Rilling and colleagues' fMRI study of social cooperation. You'll use the same payoff matrix and face the same computer partner as their Experiment 1 participants.

How the game works

You and a partner each choose, privately and at the same time, to Cooperate or Defect. How much money each of you earns in a round depends on both choices.

You are Player A, and your choices are the columns. Your partner is Player B, and their choices are the rows. In each square, the bold amount is what you earn and the amount in (parentheses) is what your partner earns.

Some examples over a 20-round game

  • If you both cooperate every round, you each earn $40.
  • If you both defect every round, you each earn only $20.
  • If one player cooperates every round while the other defects every round (unlikely), the defector earns $60 and the cooperator earns $0.

Your goal is to adopt a strategy that maximizes your earnings. In the study, participants were paid in direct proportion to their accumulated total.

How a round works: you have up to 10 seconds to choose. When you press a button, your choice lights up above your column. A couple of seconds later your partner's choice is revealed, and the square where your two choices meet is highlighted.

Important: once you press Cooperate or Defect, your choice is final. You cannot change it for that round.

Quick check

Answer both questions before you play.

1. In one round, you cooperate and your partner defects. How much do you earn?
2. Over a whole game, which outcome gives both players together the most money?

The reveal: who were you playing?

Your partner was the exact computer strategy from Rilling et al.'s Experiment 1. It defected on round 1, then played tit-for-tat: on every later round it simply copied your choice from the previous round. (Despite what the instructions said, it never used probabilities.)

Tit-for-tat is "nice but retaliatory": it rewards cooperation with cooperation and punishes defection with defection. In Axelrod's (1984) computer tournaments it outscored far more elaborate strategies. Starting with a defection, as Rilling's version did, means mutual cooperation can only begin after you extend an olive branch and take a $0 "sucker" payoff once.
